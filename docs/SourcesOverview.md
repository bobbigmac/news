Understood. The question is the character and trustworthiness of the actual publishers, not how to organise an investigation. My earlier answers went off in the wrong direction.

My angle is that **trustworthiness is primarily about the relationship between a publisher's institutional incentives, editorial behaviour, subject competence and willingness to be accountable.** Political orientation is relevant, but it isn't a substitute for any of those.

Looking at the publishers represented in your sources, there are some particularly interesting distinctions.

## **1. The institutional news organisations**

These are generally the strongest candidates for a high baseline of factual reliability, although they have quite different institutional weaknesses.

**Reuters and Associated Press**

I'd put both in a high-confidence category for conventional factual reporting. They have explicit editorial standards, substantial reporting infrastructure, established correction practices and a business model built partly around supplying journalism to other publishers.

Reuters has its Trust Principles, explicitly addressing independence and freedom from bias. AP has particularly explicit requirements for source attribution, conflicts of interest and transparent corrections.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://reutersagency.com\&sz=32)

reutersagency.com+2



One important distinction: their high reliability doesn't mean their output is independently corroborated. A large number of apparently independent publications can simply be repeating the same Reuters or AP story.

**BBC, NPR, PBS, CBC, ABC News, DW, France 24, RTE, Yle**

These deserve reasonably strong baseline confidence, but their ownership, funding and accountability arrangements differ.

The BBC is an interesting example. Ofcom's 2024–25 assessment found that the BBC had largely complied with its due impartiality and accuracy requirements since 2017–18, while also recording three breaches in six investigations over that period. That's considerably more useful evidence than simply calling the BBC trustworthy or biased.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.ofcom.org.uk\&sz=32)

www\.ofcom.org.uk



The 2026 Reuters Institute UK research also puts the BBC, ITV, Channel 4 and Financial Times among the country's most trusted news brands. This is public perception, not an objective accuracy measurement.&#x20;

**The Guardian and The Economist**

Both have interesting institutional protections, but neither is ideologically neutral.

The Guardian has the Scott Trust ownership structure, established specifically to protect its editorial and financial independence.&#x20;

The Economist has a strong and explicit editorial philosophy rooted in classical liberalism, free markets and open societies. It has unusual ownership protections and an institutional commitment to fact-checking. Its ideological perspective is readily identifiable, which is a different question from its factual reliability.&#x20;

## **2. The more problematic mainstream commercial publishers**

There are some sources where I would expect a greater tension between commercial incentives and editorial reliability.

**Daily Mail / MailOnline, Express, New York Post, Fox News**

These are not interchangeable, but they are worth examining for sensationalism, selective framing, source quality and the boundary between reporting and commentary.

Fox News has something particularly significant in its record: the Delaware court proceedings over Dominion established that the challenged election-fraud statements were false and defamatory. The separate question of actual malice remained for trial before the settlement. This is substantial, documented evidence of a serious editorial failure on a major political story, rather than just a disagreement about political orientation.&#x20;

## **4. Science and specialist publications**

I think this is one of the most important areas for distinguishing apparently reputable sources.

Consider these publications:

- Nature
- Scientific American
- Science News
- New Scientist
- Phys.org
- Medical Xpress
- ScienceDaily
- PsyPost
- Futurity
- ScienceAlert
- Live Science

They have very different relationships with the underlying research.

**Nature** is especially interesting because its journalism and research publishing are explicitly separated. Nature's journalists operate independently from the teams assessing research manuscripts and from the publisher's commercial interests. Its peer-review policy also distinguishes between different types of contribution: not everything published under the Nature brand is peer-reviewed.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.nature.com\&sz=32)

Nature+1



**ScienceDaily** provides a particularly clear example of a publication whose reputation needs to be understood through its content-production model.

Its own editorial policy says it normally works with institutional public information officers, makes few substantive changes to submitted releases, and routinely rewrites headlines and summaries.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://sciencedaily.com\&sz=32)

ScienceDaily



That is not necessarily dishonesty. But it means that a ScienceDaily article should not automatically be treated as independently researched science journalism.

**Medical Xpress** describes itself as employing editors to curate its medical coverage. That is useful, but editorial curation and independent scientific verification are different things.&#x20;

## **5. Financial and business publications**

I'd distinguish at least three rather different classes here.

**Established financial journalism:** Financial Times, Bloomberg, Wall Street Journal, Economist, CNBC.

These have substantial reporting capability, specialist expertise and access to primary financial information. They still have editorial perspectives, commercial relationships and potential conflicts.

**Industry and specialist business publications:** Finextra, PYMNTS, Banking Dive, Retail Dive, Mining Weekly, Engineering News, Stockhead, Business Recorder.

These can be extremely useful sources within their respective industries. Their expertise may exceed that of a general newspaper on particular technical or commercial matters.

However, industry dependence, advertiser relationships, corporate access and press-release dependence are worth examining carefully.

**Investment commentary and market-oriented publishers:** Seeking Alpha, MarketBeat, Nasdaq, The Motley Fool, MarketWatch.

These require differentiation between independent reporting, market data, analyst commentary, investment recommendations and contributor-generated content.

A source can be very useful for financial information while being a poor source for independent investment judgement.

## **6. Technology, gaming and entertainment publications**

I'd give specialist competence considerably more weight here.

Consider Ars Technica, The Register, IEEE Spectrum, Wired, TechCrunch, The Verge, PC Gamer, Eurogamer, Rock Paper Shotgun, GameSpot and IGN.

These publications have different editorial identities and degrees of technical depth.

Some are particularly strong at original technical reporting. Others excel at industry news, reviews, entertainment coverage or access journalism.

The distinction I'd make is between:

- Original investigation and technical analysis.
- Informed specialist reporting.
- Industry journalism based on interviews and announcements.
- Rewritten product announcements.
- Affiliate-driven buying guides and promotional content.

For example, a publication might be a perfectly reasonable source for video game release information but a relatively weak source for independently evaluating claims made by a game publisher.

That is a difference in competence and independence, not necessarily honesty.

## **7. Platforms, individual authors and unconventional publishers**

Reddit, Hacker News, Dev.to, Medium, Substack, GitHub and Twitter are particularly different from conventional publishers.

I would be very reluctant to assign these platforms a single editorial credibility rating.

There are credible specialists publishing on Substack, technical experts publishing on Dev.to, and excellent primary technical evidence on GitHub.

There is also enormous variation in the quality of individual contributions.

The individual author, their track record, expertise, incentives, corroboration and demonstrated willingness to correct errors matter far more than the platform's brand.

## **My central distinction**

I think your existing behavioural metrics are addressing an important part of the problem, but I would keep five underlying questions separate:

| Question                 | What it tells us                                                  |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Is the source competent? | Can it understand and accurately report this subject?             |
| Is it independent?       | What interests might influence its output?                        |
| Is it honest?            | Does it knowingly misrepresent, distort or conceal?               |
| Is it rigorous?          | Does it distinguish evidence, inference, speculation and opinion? |
| Is it accountable?       | Does it acknowledge errors and provide meaningful corrections?    |

There is a particularly important difference between **a source that is biased, a source that is incompetent, a source that is commercially exploitative, and a source that is deliberately deceptive**.

Those are different failure modes and shouldn't produce identical assessments.

My strongest concern with simple trust rankings is that they can inadvertently reward a publication for having an established reputation, regardless of how it actually behaves, while overlooking smaller publishers that produce exceptionally good original reporting.

Conversely, institutional prestige, professional presentation and specialist subject matter are not evidence of honesty.

I would be particularly interested in identifying sources that are *reliably useful but institutionally compromised*, and sources that are *nominally independent but have consistently poor editorial practices*. Those are two very different kinds of trust problem.
