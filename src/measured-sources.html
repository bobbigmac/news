Methodology

What “measured quality” means here

This is a measure of headline value, not a truth score. It asks whether a headline usually tells you something concrete — what happened, where, when, with what detail, consequence, context or evidence — and whether it does so without leaning on teasing, search-capture phrasing, celebrity framing or sensational language.

Each spiderweb shows eleven measured traits. The middle ring is typical for the measured sources as a group. Further out means more of the desirable trait; closer to the centre means less. All axes share one scale derived from the spread actually observed in this measured group so real differences remain visible without making axes incomparable. These are relative positions within the measured group, not absolute marks out of 100. The traits are measured after comparing sources with broadly similar story mixes, so a games outlet is not automatically penalised for covering games and a science outlet is not automatically rewarded for covering science.

The headline score combines those cues in their original units before rescaling: a headline pattern with several concrete details counts for more than one with a single cue, and packaging problems subtract from that total. Small samples are pulled towards the group average using an amount estimated from the data rather than chosen by hand. Before the cues are combined, any unusually large single-trait difference is capped at the 95th percentile of the spread actually observed across the measured group, so one trait cannot swamp the rest of the profile. The final number is then centred on the measured group so it can be displayed on a readable scale; it is an ordering aid within this group, not a universal percentage of quality.

What raises the score

  • Clear events, actions and decisions
  • Useful numbers, times, places and consequences
  • Causal links, evidence/document/testimony cues, context and explanation

What lowers it

  • Curiosity-gap teasing or withholding the central point
  • Direct-address/search-capture packaging, even when the article may still be useful
  • Sensational, celebrity and personal-life framing

Sources appear in one score ordering, with the strongest and weakest twenty pulled out for orientation. The ordering is not precise at the level of individual positions: a source a few places above another is often effectively tied with it, and each card's detail view shows the sampling-uncertainty range for its score. Every source with enough pooled headlines to score is included; small samples are marked and their scores should be read as unstable leads rather than settled verdicts. Regional editions or publisher sections are combined where they are effectively the same publication stream; unlike-owned outlets that merely share a brand are not combined.

Known limits and coverage gaps

  • The measurement cannot tell whether a claim is true, important, fairly framed or verified in the article body.
  • Only sources with enough measured headlines appear. Fox News and Fox-branded local stations currently do not have enough separate measured material here, and the affiliates are not treated as Fox News because ownership differs.
  • Small samples remain fragile even above the inclusion threshold, especially for sources with syndicated, official-release, obituary or transcript-heavy streams.
  • English-language cues do not translate cleanly to non-English sources.
  • Research-release language can look like strong evidence use; descriptive journal article titles and narrative science writing can look weaker than the underlying work really is.
  • Article summaries are used as supporting evidence where available, but coverage varies by source and feed, so they do not determine the headline score.

The machine-readable source measurements are available as measured-sources.json.
